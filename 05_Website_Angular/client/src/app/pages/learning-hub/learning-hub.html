Resume basics

Start here if this is your first resume, or your first in a long time.

A resume is not an autobiography. It is a one-page argument that you can do a specific job. Everything on it should serve that argument, and anything that doesn't is taking up space.

  • One page is plenty for a first job. Nobody is impressed by length.
  • Start each bullet with a verb: Built, Served, Organised, Assisted, Handled.
  • Put numbers in where you honestly can — "handled 40+ enquiries per shift" says more than "excellent customer service".
  • Most recent thing first, in every section.
  • Use a real email address. juan.delacruz@gmail.com, not cutiepie_2003@…
Write thisHandled 40+ customer enquiries daily across phone, email and live chat for a US client account.
Not thisHardworking team player with excellent communication skills and a passion for excellence.

The second one describes a personality. The first one describes evidence. Only one of those can be checked.

What an ATS actually is

Applicant Tracking System — the software that reads your resume before a person does.

Most medium and large employers in the Philippines now run applications through screening software first. It pulls text out of your file, looks for the skills and qualifications in the job posting, and ranks you. If it can't read your file properly, you can be filtered out before anyone human sees you — not because you're unqualified, but because of a formatting problem.

Why this platform works differently

What you type into the builder is scored exactly as you typed it — nothing lost in translation, and you can correct any of it. That is the number a recruiter ranks by.

If you also attach your own resume file, software reads that too and scores it separately, so you can see what a parser makes of your document. The two numbers are shown side by side. A gap between them is the point: it is what an ordinary screening system would have missed. Your ranking still comes from the resume you typed, so a badly-read scan cannot push you down a list — but it will show you a document worth reformatting before you send it somewhere that has no such safeguard.

The one optional exception: if you pick "Upload & analyze", an AI reads that one file — with your consent — purely to prefill the builder so you don't retype everything. You review and correct every field before saving, and the score still comes only from the structured data you approve, never from the file itself.

But you'll apply elsewhere too, so it's worth knowing the rules:

  • Use ordinary section headings and nothing clever. The ones screening software is built to recognise are Professional Summary, Work Experience, Education, Skills, Projects, Certifications, Languages and References — which is exactly what this builder prints, whichever design you pick. "My Journey" is not one of them.
  • Avoid tables, text boxes, columns, and headers/footers — parsers scramble them.
  • Don't put your contact details inside an image or a graphic.
  • Write skills out in full at least once. "Applicant Tracking System (ATS)", not just "ATS".
  • Save as PDF unless the posting specifically asks for Word.
  • Mirror the posting's own words. If it says "guest relations", write "guest relations", not "customer-facing experience".

Writing the skills section

The single biggest part of your compatibility score — half of it by default, and more on openings that weight skills harder.

Be specific. The engine — and a real ATS — matches skill against skill. A vague phrase matches nothing.

ScoresJavaScript · HTML/CSS · CRM Software · Data Entry · Bookkeeping · Customer Service
Scores nothingComputer skills · Good with people · Fast learner · Hard worker · Team player
  • List both technical and soft skills — postings ask for both, and both are scored.
  • Only claim what you could actually demonstrate if asked in an interview.
  • Open any posting and look at the crossed-out skills — those are the exact ones you're missing.
  • Add a skill the moment you learn it, even from a short course or an OJT.

The builder shows you which skills Clark Outsourcing is currently asking for, ranked by how many openings need them. That list is the shortest path to a higher score.

When you have no experience

You almost certainly have more than you think.

"No experience" usually means "no full-time job yet". That's not the same thing. All of these are legitimate experience entries:

  • OJT and internships — including unpaid ones
  • Your capstone or thesis project, if you built something
  • Part-time or seasonal work, even unrelated to the role
  • Officer roles in a student organisation
  • Freelance or commissioned work
  • Helping run a family business

And if you truly have none: apply anyway. Several openings at Clark Outsourcing ask for zero months, and where they do, the experience check costs you nothing at all — your skills and education carry the rest.

Projects, languages, and references

Optional sections. They change nothing about your score — and quite a lot about how you read.

Your compatibility score comes from three things only: your skills, your education level, and your months of experience. These sections are none of those, so adding them cannot move the number by a single point. Be clear-eyed about that. What they change is the person on the other end, who reads the page after the software has ranked it.

Projects

Often the strongest section a fresh graduate has. A capstone, an OJT output, a system you built for a barangay or a student organisation, freelance work — anything where you can name what you made and what it did. Say what it was for and how big it was: "replaced a paper logbook for about 1,200 residents" tells a recruiter more than "web development project".

If a project taught you a skill, put that skill in the Skills list as well. That is the part that scores.

Languages

Written as words — Native, Fluent, Conversational, Basic. Never as stars or a filled bar: a graphic means nothing to screening software and nothing at all in a black-and-white printout. For hospitality and front-office roles around Clark this section is worth more than it looks.

References

Ask the person first. You are putting someone else's name, employer and phone number on a document you will send to strangers, and that is their decision to make, not yours. If you would rather not, leave the section empty — nothing is printed, not even the heading, and no recruiter counts its absence against you. "Available upon request" is fine too, and takes less of the page.

All three live on the More sections step of the builder, and any section you leave empty is left off the printed page entirely.

Templates and designs

Two separate choices. The template decides what a recruiter reads first; the design decides what the page looks like.

Template — the order of your sections

  • Chronological — history first, most recent at the top. Use it when your education and experience already point at the role you want.
  • Functional — skills first, history second. Use it when you're changing direction, or when your skills are stronger than your work history.
  • Combination — equal weight to both. A reasonable default if you're unsure.

Design — the look of the page

There are fourteen, and the honest answer is that it matters less than it feels like it should. Every one is a single column of real text with standard headings and ordinary fonts, so screening software reads them identically and your score is exactly the same either way. Pick the one you would be comfortable handing to a person.

A few are worth knowing about:

  • Traditional and Classic — the most conservative. Government, banking, legal, education.
  • Compact — tighter spacing, for when a long history will not fit on one page.
  • Senior — a larger name block with your headline beneath it. For supervisory roles.
  • Graduate and Technical — these two also change the order. Graduate puts Education, Projects and Certifications first, for applicants whose schooling is their strongest card. Technical leads with Skills and Projects. Because they set their own order they override the template choice above, and the builder says so on screen when that is happening.

Switching either one never loses anything you typed. Try a few and watch the live preview beside the form.

Reading your compatibility score

The formula is published, not hidden. You see exactly what the recruiter sees.

Your score is three checks against what the posting actually asks for: the skills you list, your education level, and your months of experience. Nothing else on your resume is scored.

How much each of the three is worth is set per opening. Most use the site default — {{ W.skills * 100 }}% skills, {{ W.education * 100 }}% education, {{ W.experience * 100 }}% experience, the study’s initial weights, refined through testing. A recruiter can weight a particular role differently: a developer post might lean harder on skills, a trainee post on education. Whatever an opening uses is printed on the opening itself and in your dashboard breakdown, so you are never scored on a split you were not shown. No criterion can be switched off entirely — each one is always worth at least 10%.

One consequence worth knowing: because two openings can weight things differently, their percentages are not strictly comparable. A 70% on one posting and a 70% on another can mean different things. Read the breakdown, not just the number.

Below {{ T.related }}%Related roles get suggested instead. You are never turned away.

That bottom row is the part that matters most. On this platform a low score is never a rejection. If your resume doesn't fit a posting, the system points you at roles that do fit the skills you already have — and your application still lands on the recruiter's list either way. Common mistakes

Things that quietly cost people interviews.

  • Sending the same resume everywhere. Keep a version per role type — the builder lets you save as many as you want.
  • Leaving a section blank. An empty heading looks like an oversight. Remove it instead.
  • Listing skills you can't demonstrate. It falls apart in the interview, and it's worse than an honest gap.
  • Burying the good part. Your best evidence goes at the top of its section, not the bottom.
  • Not proofreading. Read it out loud once. Typos in the first two lines are the ones people notice.
  • Applying only to perfect matches. Recruiters read the whole list, not just the top of it.
